# Soundproofing a Studio by Noisy Bruckner Blvd — What We Learned at 1389 Kearney

If you've ever stood on Kearney Ave at 2 PM and felt a semi truck rattle your chest cavity from Bruckner Boulevard, you know why this blog exists. We built a recording studio 200 feet from one of the loudest corridors in the Bronx — and somehow, it works.

_See photos of the space at mohikontok.com_

## The Noise Problem

Bruckner Blvd isn't just traffic. It's a six-lane artery feeding the Bruckner Expressway, carrying tractor-trailers, ambulances, delivery trucks, and the occasional drunk at 3 AM who thinks revving their engine is a personality trait. On top of that, we're under the LaGuardia runway 13/31 approach — so every 90 seconds, a plane drops lower and the walls hum.

We measured **72-78 dB** outside the building on a typical afternoon. Inside the original space? **58-62 dB**. For context, a professional recording booth needs ambient noise below 30 dB, ideally NC-15 or lower. We had work to do.

## The Three Enemies: Airborne, Structure-Borne, and Low Frequency

Sound travels two ways through a building. **Airborne noise** — trucks, voices, planes — enters through gaps, windows, vents. **Structure-borne noise** — vibrations from the street, subway, and machinery — travels through the concrete, steel, and wood like a guitar string carries a pluck to the tuning peg.

And then there's the real killer: **low frequencies**. Bass doesn't care about foam panels. A 60 Hz rumble from a truck engine will go through drywall like it's tissue paper. Low frequencies have long wavelengths — a 60 Hz wave is roughly 19 feet long — which means they bend around obstacles and penetrate materials that higher frequencies bounce off.

## What We Did — Layer by Layer

### 1\. Decoupled Walls (The Mass-Spring-Mass System)

The single most effective thing we did was build **a second set of walls** inside the existing ones, separated by a 1-inch air gap. This is called decoupling, and it breaks the mechanical path that vibrations use to travel from the street through the building structure into your room.

We used resilient channels — metal hat channels mounted on isolation clips — to attach 5/8" Type X drywall to the new stud wall. The clips act like shock absorbers, absorbing vibration before it reaches the drywall surface. This created a mass-spring-mass system: existing wall (mass) → air gap + resilient channel (spring) → new drywall (mass).

**Cost:** ~$3,200 for resilient clips, channels, and drywall for a 12x16 room.

### 2\. Green Glue Between Drywall Layers

On top of the decoupled wall, we added a second layer of 5/8" drywall with **Green Glue** sandwiched between. Green Glue is a viscoelastic damping compound — it stays slightly tacky forever and converts sound energy into trace amounts of heat.

The key number: Green Glue at 2 tubes per 4x8 sheet can reduce sound transmission by an additional 5-8 STC points. That's the difference between "I can hear every word" and "I can't tell someone is talking next door."

**Cost:** ~$600 for Green Glue + second drywall layer.

### 3\. Mass-Loaded Vinyl (MLV) on the Ceiling

The ceiling was the weakest link. We stapled 2 lb/sq ft **mass-loaded vinyl** to the ceiling joists before installing the resilient channel and double drywall. MLV is a flexible, dense rubber sheet — it adds mass without adding rigidity, blocking sound without creating new vibration paths.

**Cost:** ~$450 for a 12x16 ceiling.

### 4\. Solid Core Door with Drop Seal

We replaced the hollow interior door with a **solid-core wood door** and added an automatic drop seal. The drop seal presses down against the threshold when the door closes, eliminating the 1/2-inch gap that ruins an otherwise soundproof wall. A 1% gap can reduce a wall's STC rating by 10 points.

**Cost:** ~$350 for door + drop seal.

### 5\. Sealing Every Gap with Acoustic Caulk

Every seam, every outlet box, every corner — sealed with **acoustic sealant**. Not regular caulk. Acoustic caulk stays flexible permanently and maintains its seal as the building shifts. We went through 12 tubes. Every electrical box got a putty pad.

**Cost:** ~$120 for caulk + putty pads.

## The Results

After the build, we measured ambient noise inside the tracking room at **27-29 dB** — down from 58-62 dB. That's a reduction of roughly 30 dB. On the STC scale, we went from approximately STC 35 to approximately STC 55+.

Does Bruckner still exist? Absolutely. Can you hear a truck rumble at 60 Hz if you listen hard enough with headphones off? Sometimes, faintly. But put on headphones, hit record, and it's gone. The room works.

## What We'd Do Differently

If we had to do it again, I'd float the floor. We used cork underlayment and OSB on the existing slab, which helped with impact noise but didn't fully decouple structure-borne vibration. A proper floated floor would have cost another $800 and knocked out another 3-5 dB of low-frequency rumble.

I'd also skip the foam panels people keep recommending. Foam absorbs high frequencies and makes a room sound less echoey — it does **not** block sound. We learned this the expensive way.
