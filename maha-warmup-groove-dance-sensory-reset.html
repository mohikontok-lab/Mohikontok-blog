Warm-Up Groove: Dance as Sensory Reset for Neurodivergent Creators

Maha Biswas · June 26, 2026 · 4 min read
dancesensoryneurodivergentwarm-upbronx

Starting my day with a 5-minute stim-dance in the lab. Movement is my sensory reset button — it grounds me, sharpens my focus, and fuels the tutorials I create for neurodivergent creators.

If you're on the spectrum like me, you know the feeling: too much noise, too many lights, too many inputs, and your brain goes fuzzy. Dance isn't just art for us — it's a regulated sensory input that helps manage overload and keep us present.

My 4-Step Warm-Up

I do this before every recording session at Mohikontok Sound Lab. Five minutes. No music required — just your body and your breath.

  1. Body roll — Slow, from feet to head. Releases tension you didn't know you were holding. Imagine a wave moving through you, like the Bronx River after rain.
  2. Shoulder shimmy — Quick, rhythmic. Sparks circulation and wakes up your upper body. I think of it as shaking off the static.
  3. Foot tap sync — Tap to a metronome beat (60 BPM is perfect). This is the secret weapon — it trains your brain to lock onto a rhythm, which makes everything in the studio feel more natural.
  4. Full-body spin — One spin, eyes open. Reboots the nervous system. Sounds weird, works every time.

Try it and notice how your brain feels clearer afterward. Not "fixed" — clearer. Like someone turned down the static on a radio and you can hear the song underneath.

Why This Works for Autistic Creators

As someone diagnosed with Asperger's in 7th grade, I've spent years learning what my sensory system needs. Dance gives me proprioceptive input — awareness of where my body is in space — which is something a lot of autistic people crave. The rhythmic movement also provides vestibular stimulation, the same kind you get from swinging or bouncing.

The sound lab at 1389 Kearney Ave is my sensory-safe space. The soundproofing that Edan Brown writes about? That's not just for clean recordings — it's what makes the room calm enough for someone like me to actually think in.

Before You Record

Next time you're about to record vocals, a podcast, or a tutorial — don't just sit down and start. Take five minutes. Do the warm-up. Your nervous system will thank you, and your audio will sound better because you're more grounded and present.
