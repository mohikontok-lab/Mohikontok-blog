Bass Travels Through Everything: Why Low Frequencies Are the Hardest to Kill

Edan Brown · 2026-06-29 · 5 min read
soundproofinglow-frequencybassstudiocreators

The room felt like a drum that never stopped vibrating," says Maha Biswas, Bronx‑based content creator, "until I finally tamed the low end. Now my podcasts keep listeners glued, not bouncing out for the next beat." That's the hook: low frequencies are the sneaky culprits that bleed through walls, floors, and ceilings, turning a crisp vocal track into a muddy mess that drives viewers away. In this post I unpack why those rumbling 60‑120 Hz tones are so stubborn and what we did at Mohikontok Sound Lab to lock them down without breaking the bank.

The physics of low‑frequency bleed

Low‑frequency sound waves have long wavelengths—think about a 50 Hz tone whose wavelength stretches nearly 7 m. Because they’re so long, they can wrap around obstacles, diffract through tiny gaps, and make their way through structural elements that block higher frequencies. In a typical home studio, even a well‑sealed wall may let the bass travel via studs, floor joists, or the air cavity, resulting in a measured STC rating that looks good on paper but fails when you play a kick drum.

Why traditional mass isn’t enough

Adding drywall or dense material improves high‑frequency isolation, but low frequencies need mass and damping. The mass‑spring‑mass principle we used in our Bruckner Blvd build adds a decoupled air cavity between two layers of 5/8" gypsum, which shifts the resonant frequency lower and reduces transmission. However, without a viscoelastic layer like Green Glue, the system can still vibrate at its own resonant frequency, leaking energy.

What we actually did

  • Decoupled double walls – two layers of 5/8" drywall separated by 2 in. resilient channel studs, creating a ~4 in. air gap.
  • Green Glue damping – applied between the drywall layers, dissipating vibrational energy as heat.
  • Mass‑Loaded Vinyl (MLV) on the ceiling – 1/8" MLV adds about 1 lb/ft², crucial for the ceiling where low‑frequency energy often leaks upward.
  • Bass traps in corners – DIY broadband traps using 4 in. fiberglass wrapped in fabric, tuned to absorb 60‑120 Hz.
  • Sealing every gap – acoustic caulk around studs, foam backer rod in expansion gaps, and door sweeps on the studio entry.

Quick wins for creators on a budget

  • Portable bass traps: Heavy‑weight moving blankets hung in corners can absorb up to 10 dB of low‑frequency energy.
  • DIY MLV alternative: Thick rubber flooring under a rug can add mass without the cost of commercial MLV.
  • Seal the door: Even a simple PVC door sweep can cut low‑freq leakage by 5‑7 dB.

Takeaway you can apply today

Start with the easiest fix: seal every gap you can see—around windows, doors, and where the wall meets the floor. Then add a couple of corner bass traps using affordable fiberglass. If you can, invest in a thin layer of Green Glue between any existing double‑wall construction; the improvement in low‑frequency isolation is immediate.

“The difference is night‑and‑day. I can finally record my voice‑overs without that endless low‑end boom,” says Maha Biswas, content creator and social media strategist.
